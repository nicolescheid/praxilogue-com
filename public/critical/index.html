Thinking is Critical · Module 2

Addressing Biases

Most advice about working with AI concerns the machine. Write better prompts. Give more context. Check the output. All of it is sound, and all of it assumes the person at the keyboard is a fixed quantity.

They are not. The questions you ask are shaped by what you already believe. The parts of an answer you keep are shaped by the same thing. Two people can send the same model the same problem and come away with opposite conclusions, both of them satisfied, neither of them aware of what they filtered on the way through.

This module is about that filter.

The human practice underneath it: everyone knows the colleague who only ever runs an idea past the person most likely to agree with it. They are not being dishonest. They want the idea to work, so they take it to the person who will help it work. AI makes that colleague of everyone, because it is always available, always willing, and never visibly annoyed by the fifth attempt to get the answer you wanted.

How this page works. Each section opens with something to try before the explanation, because the explanation lands differently once you have caught yourself doing the thing. The exercises run in your browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere; it is kept on this device so the closing card can be built from your own words. This is version one. A version that talks back is in progress.

Confirmation bias

This is a pattern match on phrasing, not a judgment. It will miss some and flag some wrongly. The point is to see the shape of what you asked.

What is happening

Most people find their own question already contains the answer. "What's the best way to structure the rollout I've planned?" carries an assumption that the rollout should happen. "Should we be rolling this out at all?" is a different question and will return a different document.

This is confirmation bias, which is the tendency to seek and weight evidence that supports what you already hold. It is well documented in humans and it predates AI by a long way. What AI changes is the speed and the compliance. A colleague who is asked a loaded question will often push back on the framing. A model is far more likely to answer the question as posed.

The effect operates in two places, and most people only notice one of them.

The first is the question. You ask in a way that invites agreement, and you get agreement.

The second is the reading. Even when the answer is balanced, you retain the parts that support your position and skim the parts that complicate it. This one is harder to catch, because you never see a record of what you skipped. The document looked supportive. You have no memory of the three paragraphs that were not.

Where most people stop

The usual fix is to fix the question, and that is worth doing. Ask what would make this wrong. Present the situation without your preferred conclusion. Both work.

The reading problem survives that fix. You can ask a perfectly neutral question, receive a genuinely balanced answer, and still walk away with a one-sided version of it.

A practice for the second half:

After you have read a response, before you act on it, name the part you found least convincing. Then ask yourself why you found it least convincing. Sometimes the reason is that the argument was weak. Sometimes the reason is that it was inconvenient. The two feel identical from the inside, and separating them takes a deliberate pass.

Asking for "both sides" does not solve this on its own. It produces a symmetrical answer, which you then read asymmetrically.

Selective adherence

Bring to mind two occasions when you took an AI's suggestion, and one occasion when you overrode it. For the one you overrode: what made you override it?

Most people report some version of "it felt wrong" or "I could tell it wasn't right". Sit with that for a moment and ask what the feeling was tracking. Then do the reading below, which makes the feeling visible.

What is happening

There is a common assumption that the risk with AI is uniform over-trust, that people defer to the machine across the board because it sounds authoritative. It is a reasonable assumption. The research is more interesting than that.

Alon-Barkat and Busuioc ran three studies looking for exactly this effect. They did not find it. Participants showed no significant difference in how much they adhered to advice depending on whether it came from an algorithm or a human expert.

What they did find: participants adhered more strongly when the advice aligned with the beliefs and stereotypes they already held. They named this selective adherence.

Trust in AI is not a level. It is a pattern. It goes up where the output agrees with you and down where it does not, and the movement is invisible because it feels like judgment. The sense of "I could tell it wasn't right" is doing real work some of the time. The rest of the time it is confirmation bias wearing the costume of expertise.

This is the load-bearing idea in the module. Everything else in it follows from this one.

Where most people stop

The natural conclusion is that you should trust AI less. Be more sceptical across the board, check more things, take less at face value.

Lowering trust uniformly leaves the pattern completely intact. If you scale your scepticism up by twenty percent everywhere, you are still more sceptical of the things you disagreed with, because that is where you were starting from. The variance is the problem, and adjusting the average does nothing to it.

The move that works runs the other way. The parts of an answer that annoyed you have already been checked, because irritation is a verification prompt. The parts you accepted without friction are the unexamined ones.

A practice: when you finish reading an AI response, mark the passages you accepted immediately and without resistance. Those are your candidates for checking. The ones you argued with have already had your attention.

This will feel backwards for a while. It is meant to.

2.3

Agreement is a feature

Try this first · an order experimentnot started

Take a position you do not personally hold and think is fairly weak. Something you could argue but would not.

Two prompts. Run them in this order in an AI of your choice, the second in a brand-new conversation. Then, another day, run them the other way round and compare.

Watch how readily it complies with the first, and how good the case looks. Then notice that the order of those two requests changes what you get. That difference is the subject of this section.

What is happening

Language models are built to continue and cooperate with what you give them. This is a structural property, present in the training objective before anyone tunes anything, and reinforced by the preference training that follows. Helpfulness, responsiveness and agreement are rewarded. Disagreement, particularly unprompted disagreement, generally is not.

There is no intent in this. The model is not flattering you, and it is not trying to keep you happy in the way a person might. It has no position of its own that it is suppressing.

It is also the same property that makes the thing useful. Consider a typewriter that argued with you about your ending. It would be unusable. A tool that resisted your direction on principle would be worse at every job you actually want done. The cooperativeness is the point, and it comes with a cost that lands entirely on you.

The practical consequence is simple and easy to state:

The model will not supply the disagreement. You have to ask for it.

Where most people stop

Once people understand this, they start asking for criticism. "What's wrong with this?" "Play devil's advocate." "Push back on me."

That is the right instinct and it is weaker than it looks, because of where the request is usually issued. If you have spent nine messages building a case, and on the tenth you ask the model to critique it, you get critique shaped by nine messages of context in which you were clearly invested in the case. The criticism arrives pre-softened. It will find the flaws you can live with.

What raises the quality of the disagreement:

Move it out of the thread.
Open a fresh conversation with no history and put the position in cold. The absence of context is the point.
Detach it from you.
"A colleague has proposed the following" produces sharper analysis than "here is my proposal", for the same reason a friend reviews your CV more honestly than they review their own.
Ask before you commit.
Request the strongest case against a position before you have stated that you hold it. Once your view is on the record, every subsequent turn is written by a model that knows it.
Name a specific critic.
A general request for criticism returns general criticism. Asking what a particular kind of sceptic would say, someone with a defined stake and a defined objection, returns something you can actually work with.

2.4

Expertise bias

For each, estimate how long you spent evaluating it, and count how many individual claims you actually verified. Not read carefully. Verified.

In your field

Outside your field

What is happening

The pattern most people find is that they spent less time on the one in their own field.

This runs against intuition. Your expertise is precisely what equips you to catch errors, so it should be the place you catch the most. What happens instead is that fluency in the vocabulary reads as correctness. The output uses the terms you use, in the arrangements you expect, and that familiarity substitutes for scrutiny. The claims that match your existing model of the field pass without inspection, which is confirmation bias operating on your professional knowledge rather than on your opinions.

Outside your field the failure is different in kind. You cannot evaluate the content because you do not have the knowledge to evaluate it against. Two responses are common. Some people defer entirely, on the grounds that they have nothing better. Others reject the whole thing on a general suspicion of AI. Both are decisions made without information.

The related trap is covered in Module 3: fluent output produces a feeling of understanding that outpaces actual understanding. Reading a clear explanation of something you could not explain yourself feels a great deal like learning it.

Where most people stop

The common takeaway is to be more careful in your own field. Worth doing, and it only addresses half of the problem.

The outside-your-field case has a solution that people rarely reach on their own, because it requires giving up on evaluating the content.

You cannot check whether a claim about a field you do not know is true. You can check a number of other things, and they are informative:

Structure.
Does the argument hold together? Do the conclusions follow from the stated reasons? Logical coherence is domain-independent and you are fully qualified to assess it.
Specificity.
Vague claims are cheap to produce. Specific ones commit to something. A response that hedges everywhere may be honest, and it may also be avoiding anything checkable.
Attributability.
Can each significant claim be traced to a source that exists and says what it is reported to say? This one is checkable regardless of expertise, and it is where fabrication is most often exposed.
Load-bearing claims.
Most answers rest on one or two claims that everything else depends on. Identify those and check only those. Verifying the whole document is usually not possible and usually not necessary.

2.5

Challenging bias

Try this first · read without rebuttingnot started

Here is the strongest case against a position most people reading this page hold: that every knowledge worker should be using AI daily.

Read it one paragraph at a time. Pay attention to what you do while reading. The moment you notice yourself composing a reply, press the button. There is no wrong answer, and nobody is watching.

Daily use is a target for the tool, not for the work. A habit measured in frequency rewards finding things to use it on, and the work that benefits most from AI is unevenly distributed. Some roles have a great deal of it. Some have almost none.
The skill that good AI use draws on, knowing what to check and what to trust, is built by doing the work unaided. A person who has never drafted the document has less to evaluate the draft against. Mandating daily use for people early in a role removes the practice that would make their use of it good.
Adoption metrics are easy to hit and hard to interpret. A team can log daily use and be producing more text with less thought in it. The survey work on cognitive effort (Lee et al., 2025) found that higher confidence in the tool went with self-reported reductions in critical engagement. Frequency is not the thing anyone wants. It is the thing that is easy to count.
The most reliable gains so far sit in a narrow band of tasks: first drafts, summaries, code with tests, transformations between formats. Outside that band the evidence is thinner and the failure modes are quieter. "Daily" pushes use into the thin part of the evidence.
None of this is an argument against AI at work. It is an argument against the word "every" and the word "daily". The defensible version is that people should use it where it helps and be able to tell where that is, which is a different and harder policy to write.

What is happening

The value in seeking out the opposing case is not primarily that it changes your mind. It usually will not, and a course that promised otherwise would be overselling.

The value is in what survives. Running a position against its strongest opposition tells you which parts of it are load-bearing and which parts you were carrying out of habit. Positions that have never been tested tend to contain a mixture of both, and their holders cannot tell which is which.

This has a long history outside AI. Steelmanning, the practice of constructing the strongest version of an opposing argument before responding to it, is standard in philosophy and competitive debate. Legal training is built around arguing assigned positions rather than held ones. The reason those disciplines do it is not fairness. It is that you do not understand your own argument until you have seen what it has to survive.

AI makes the practice cheap. Generating a serious opposing case used to require finding a person who held it and was willing to argue. Now it takes a sentence.

Where most people stop

The common version is to ask for counterarguments and then read them defensively, which produces the feeling of having tested a position without the substance of it. The rebuttals you compose while reading are the sound of the test failing.

Three things that make it real:

Ask before you declare.
Put the question as an open one before you state which side you are on. Once your position is in the thread, everything after it is generated by a model that knows where you stand. See 2.3.
Name the disconfirming evidence.
Ask yourself what specific finding would change your mind. Then ask whether that finding exists. If nothing could change your mind, you are not holding a position, and the exercise has told you something more useful than a counterargument would have.
Argue the other side yourself.
Take the opposing position and defend it for one turn, in your own words, against an AI instructed to attack it. This is harder than reading a counterargument and it is the version that works, for the same reason that writing something teaches you more than reading it.

Call-out · other humans

Where it is available to you, run an important answer past another person, particularly someone with subject matter expertise.

The value here is specific to this module. A knowledgeable colleague brings accuracy, and that matters. What they bring that no amount of careful prompting can replicate is a frame that is not yours. They will not share the assumptions you did not know you were making, because they did not make them. The question that does not occur to you is the question they ask first.

The accuracy dimension of this, including how to choose which humans and how to weigh what they tell you, is covered in Module 5.

Carrying this out of the module

  1. Your question usually contains your answer. Write the version a person who disagreed with you would ask, and notice the difference.
  2. Check the parts you accepted easily. The parts that annoyed you have already had your attention.
  3. The model will not supply disagreement. Ask for it, and ask for it outside the thread where you built your case.
  4. In your own field, slow down. Outside it, check structure, specificity and attribution instead of content.
  5. Test a position before you announce it.
